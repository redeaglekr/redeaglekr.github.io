02 / Research

Understanding stars,
exploring our Galaxy.

My research spans observational astronomy, from the detection and characterisation of exoplanets to the study of stellar oscillations and the application of asteroseismology to Galactic archaeology. I work with large photometric and spectroscopic datasets to investigate the properties, structure and evolution of stars.

01 — Background

Pre-PhD experience

My interest in planets has shaped my path as a researcher. I began exploring this interest through exoplanets, focusing on their detection and characterisation using the radial velocity (RV) method. During a summer project at the Indian Institute of Astrophysics, I re-derived the RVs of the planet-hosting star HD 189733 using HARPS (High Accuracy Radial velocity Planet Searcher) spectra. This gave me a deeper understanding of high-resolution stellar spectroscopy, echelle spectrographs, and different stages of data reduction.

For my Master's project, I carried out exoplanet detection from a complementary direction using transit photometry. Working with K2 light curves, I gained experience in retrieving light curves from the MAST archive, searching for transits, modelling transit signals and stellar limb darkening, and fitting models using Python.

My research then moved from focusing on individual systems to large datasets of objects. As a Junior Research Fellow at the Indian Institute of Astrophysics, I used machine-learning techniques to classify sources as stars, galaxies, or quasars using photometric magnitudes from SDSS (Sloan Digital Sky Survey) and GALEX (Galaxy Evolution Explorer). This introduced me to SQL (Structured Query Language), feature engineering, different machine-learning algorithms and the analysis of large datasets.

I then moved to Israel, where I resumed my work on exoplanet detection and characterisation. I applied a likelihood-estimation-based algorithm to approximately fifteen years of RV measurements from archival HARPS observations to detect exoplanets. Among several detections, we identified two promising cold Jupiters orbiting the subgiant star HD 103891 and the main-sequence star HD 105779 (Sreenivas et al. 2022).

This work strengthened my knowledge of RV analysis and statistical inference, including likelihood minimisation, hypothesis testing, sampling techniques and Gaussian Process modelling of stellar activity.

In parallel, I gained experience with ground-based photometric observations at the WISE Observatory and served as a Co-Investigator on several observing proposals with the Las Cumbres Observatory (LCO) network. I extracted and detrended LCO photometry to search for transits of CARMENES RV exoplanet candidates and to measure stellar rotation periods. These results contributed to several publications (Caballero et al. 2022; Enric et al. 2023; Jorge et al. 2025).

Together, these experiences allowed me to work across different aspects of observational astronomy, from data acquisition to analysing large datasets and modelling RV and photometric time series. Over the course of my research, working on planets made me realise that understanding them requires an equally detailed understanding of their host stars. This naturally drew me towards stellar physics and motivated my PhD research, where asteroseismology offered a way to probe stars in much greater detail.

02 — Doctoral research

PhD contributions

During my PhD, I studied the properties of stellar oscillations and used them to characterise stars and improve our understanding of the Galaxy. My work gradually progressed from developing a method to measure asteroseismic parameters from Kepler light curves, to understanding oscillations and granulation at different wavelengths, to analysing tens of thousands of stars observed by TESS (Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite), and eventually to the asteroseismic characterisation of an ultra metal-poor star.

The following projects highlight this progression and the expertise I developed during my PhD.

Project 01

A simple method to measure νmax for asteroseismology

Two fundamental diagnostics of solar-like oscillations are the frequency of maximum power (νmax) and the large frequency separation (Δν). When combined with precise effective temperatures and parallaxes from Gaia, νmax alone can provide strong constraints on stellar parameters.

With the large number of light curves expected from upcoming space missions such as Roman (Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope) and PLATO (PLAnetary Transits and Oscillations of stars), there is a need for simple and robust methods that can be applied to large stellar samples. A fast and simple method was therefore developed to measure νmax without removing the granulation background.

Previous studies have shown that different choices of background models generally produce νmax values within the measurement uncertainties. The resulting stellar radii were compared with independent estimates from Gaia, showing slightly reduced discrepancies and improved precision. The analysis further demonstrated that granulation does not significantly affect the measurement of νmax or the resulting stellar radii.

This provides a simple approach for deriving fundamental stellar properties, including mass, radius and surface gravity, from stellar oscillations (Sreenivas et al. 2024).

Project 02

Testing the wavelength dependence of oscillation and granulation using Kepler and TESS

Kjeldsen & Bedding (2011) proposed that the power associated with oscillations and granulation should scale with the wavelength of the passband in a similar way. Since solar-like oscillations and granulation are both associated with near-surface convection, their amplitudes (or power) and their variations are expected to be closely related.

The different passbands of Kepler and TESS provide an opportunity to test this wavelength dependence observationally. Red giants observed by both missions were used to compare the oscillation and granulation signals measured at different wavelengths.

The analysis showed that both oscillation and granulation power decrease towards the redder TESS passband, with the TESS-to-Kepler power ratios agreeing with theoretical predictions (Lund et al. 2019). Importantly, the mean power ratios for oscillations and granulation were also found to agree with each other, providing observational evidence that the two signals have a similar wavelength dependence. The analysis further showed no clear dependence of these ratios on the stellar parameters considered in the study.

An important outcome of this work was that amplitude measurements are sensitive to the choice of light-curve product and the way the photometry is extracted and detrended. Different TESS light-curve products can produce different measured amplitudes, highlighting the need for particular care when studying stellar variability through amplitude measurements.

This work therefore involved extensive testing of TESS pixel-level data, light-curve extraction and detrending, along with binary-star modelling using PHOEBE and the calculation of bolometric corrections from stellar atmosphere models.

The results also motivated questions that are directly relevant to future Roman observations in the infrared wavelengths, where both oscillation and granulation signals are weaker. This information could help mitigate stellar variability in the detection and characterisation of planets. More generally, this study highlights the value of simultaneous multi-band observations for understanding and characterising stellar oscillations and granulation (Sreenivas et al. 2025).

Project 03

Red giants in the TESS Continuous Viewing Zones: Global asteroseismology of 19,000 oscillating red giants

The last two decades have demonstrated the power of space-based photometry for detecting solar-like oscillations in large numbers of stars. In particular, the long and nearly continuous observations from Kepler enabled precise asteroseismic characterisation of red giants, providing stellar masses, radii and surface gravities with typical precisions of 7%, 2.4% and 0.01 dex, respectively (Yu et al. 2018).

TESS has extended this opportunity to stars across almost the entire sky, although its shorter observing baseline generally limits the quality of asteroseismic measurements. The TESS Continuous Viewing Zones (CVZs), however, provide light curves spanning multiple years and therefore offer an opportunity to achieve Kepler-like precision.

The aim of this project was to identify oscillating red giants in the TESS CVZs and determine their global asteroseismic properties. Starting from a sample of about 72,000 stars, I carried out sample selection, light-curve detrending, oscillation detection, manual vetting to ensure quality, and the measurement of global asteroseismic parameters.

The final catalogue contains 19,151 oscillating red giants, making it the largest catalogue of TESS red giants with homogeneously determined asteroseismic parameters to date. The measured νmax and Δν values have typical precisions of 1.5% and 1.0%, respectively.

Combined with spectroscopic parameters, they provide stellar masses and radii with typical precisions of 7.5% and 2.8%, comparable to those achieved using four years of Kepler observations. The resulting seismic radii also show excellent agreement with independent Gaia radii.

The resulting catalogue provides an opportunity to investigate the distribution and kinematics of red giants across the Galaxy. The analysis shows that low-mass stars lie farther from the Galactic plane and have larger velocities. These results reproduce several well-known Galactic trends and can therefore serve as an important dataset for Galactic archaeology (Sreenivas et al. 2026).

Project 04

Asteroseismology of the ultra metal-poor star BD+44° 493 using TESS

Large asteroseismic catalogues are important for improving current methodologies and testing them across different stellar populations. The resulting precise stellar parameters also provide valuable information for understanding the structure and properties of the Galactic neighbourhood.

At the same time, large samples offer the opportunity to identify unusual stars and trends that may otherwise be missed, sometimes opening new directions for investigation. Working with such a large sample led to the discovery of an extremely metal-poor star showing solar-like oscillations. This provided an opportunity to move from global asteroseismic analysis of thousands of stars to detailed modelling of an individual and unusual system.

The analysis involved extracting light curves directly from TESS Target Pixel Files (TPFs), detrending them using Co-trending Basis Vectors (CBVs), modelling individual oscillation frequencies, and performing stellar modelling in the metal-poor regime.

This work expanded my experience from large-scale asteroseismic analysis to detailed frequency and stellar modelling of individual stars (Sreenivas et al. 2026b, in preparation).
